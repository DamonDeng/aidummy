# EXP-000: Before the Experiments — Assembly

**Date:** (sometime before 2026-03)
**Status:** 📖 ORIGIN LOG
**Objective:** This is not an experiment. There are no hypotheses, no metrics, no results. This is just what it looked like before any of that started.

---

## The Parts

It begins, as all hardware projects do, with parts on a table.

Red anodized aluminum — the CNC-machined structural components of the arm laid out flat before assembly. Joint housings, link brackets, the curved shoulder frame. Silver bearing flanges. The black base cube. A yellow plastic bag in the corner containing screws.

Looking at this photo, you can see the full vocabulary of the machine before it is a machine. Each piece is precisely made — the kind of quality that only comes from proper machining, not iteration. Someone had already decided what this arm would be long before assembly day.

The tools are out. A precision screwdriver set. Small pliers. A yellow jar that might be lubricant. Connector cables still in their plastic bags.

---

## The Electronics

Another photo: the bench in mid-assembly, viewed from above. A chaotic but familiar kind of chaos — the controlled mess of someone who knows exactly where everything is.

Stepper motors. Driver boards. A Dupont connector kit. Motor controller PCBs still in anti-static bags. A spatula (always a spatula — useful for prying things apart that shouldn't have been pressed together). Jumper wires in a heap. Heat shrink tubing scattered at the edges.

A GranPro tool case. A soldering iron.

Two yellow plastic cups — one appears to be a small container, perhaps for component sorting. Yellow was apparently a theme long before the 3D-printed covers.

---

## The Cables

One photo is nothing but cables: close-up, macro. Red, green, yellow, and black wires being hand-crimped with needle-nose tweezers. The ends have bare metal crimp terminals. Heat shrink tubing cut and placed nearby.

This is tedious work — the kind that doesn't make it into project summaries. Each connector made by hand, each wire routed through the arm body so it can bend and twist a thousand times without breaking.

Looking at this photo, it's easy to forget that every single move the arm has ever made depended on these connections.

---

## The Arm Takes Shape

At some point, it becomes recognizable.

A photo of the arm standing on the desk, partially assembled. J1 through J4 complete, the upper section still open — wires emerging from the top joint like a half-finished thought. The red aluminum body on the black base. The workspace behind it: an AWS box, yellow plastic bags, more PCBs, more wire.

A MacBook visible. A plant in the corner.

It looks like a lab. A real one — not a studio, not a render. The kind of place where things get built at the cost of desk space and cable management.

---

## The Workspace

One photo steps back to show the full scene.

A long desk. The arm at the far end, upright now, most of its joints assembled. The MacBook open next to it. Between the camera and the arm: breadboards, rainbow ribbon cables, jumper wire kits, component organizers, bags of parts, more yellow cups. A pair of scissors. Everything a person needs to build something and debug it and rebuild it.

There's a label on the desk divider: **300.K2**. An office, not a garage.

In the background, another desk, a cardboard box. The kind of space that looks like it could become anything.

---

## What Came Next

After the parts came the wiring. After the wiring came the firmware. After the firmware came the first movement — probably tentative, probably wrong, then corrected. After that came a USB server, then an API, then IK solvers, then a camera, then computer vision experiments, then this log.

The arm has moved through hundreds of poses now. It's generated its own training data. It's pointed at the screen. It's survived a "PA!" during a cover swap and had all six joints tested and confirmed healthy afterward.

None of that is in these photos. These photos are from before any of that — when it was still just red aluminum and a bag of screws on a desk.

That's worth remembering.
